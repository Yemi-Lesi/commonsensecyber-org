Check if your email has appeared in a data breach

A data breach happens when a company's data is stolen — usernames, emails, and sometimes passwords end up in the hands of criminals. It happens to big, well-known companies too, and it's not your fault.

Have I Been Pwned is a free, independent tool used by security professionals worldwide. It lets you check whether your email address has shown up in a known breach — without ever asking for your password.

  • If you appear in a breach, change the password for that account — and any other account using the same password.
  • Turn on two-step verification if you haven't already (see below).
  • Appearing in a breach doesn't automatically mean your current account is compromised — but it's a good reason to double-check.

Use a password manager

A password manager creates and remembers a long, unique password for every account, so you only have to remember one. Reusing passwords is one of the biggest risks online — if one site is breached, criminals try that same password everywhere else.

Browser-based

  • Built into Chrome, Safari, Firefox, and Edge — no extra app needed.
  • Free, and syncs automatically if you're signed in.
  • A good starting point, especially if you mostly use one browser.

Dedicated apps

  • Works across every browser, app, and device — not just one.
  • Often includes extras like breach alerts and secure sharing.
  • Worth it if you use multiple browsers or devices, or want more control.

What to look for when choosing one

  • Strong, independently-audited encryption.
  • Works across the devices you actually use.
  • A clear, honest privacy policy.
  • Support for passkeys and two-step verification.

Turn on two-step verification (MFA)

Two-step verification — also called MFA or 2FA — adds a second check after your password, so a stolen password alone isn't enough to break in. It's one of the single best things you can do to protect an account.

  • Strong Authenticator app — generates a changing code on your phone.
  • Strongest Security key — a small physical device you tap or plug in.
  • Strongest Passkey — see below. Learn more
  • Weaker, but still helpful Text message (SMS) codes — better than nothing, but codes can sometimes be intercepted.
  • Backup only Backup codes — not for everyday use; save these somewhere safe in case you lose access.

What's a passkey?

A passkey replaces your password with your device's fingerprint, face, or screen lock. Instead of typing anything, you approve the sign-in on a device you already trust.

Passkeys are built to resist phishing: they're tied to the exact website or app they were created for, so a fake look-alike site simply can't use them — even if it tricks you into visiting.

Look for "Passkey" in the security settings of accounts like Google, Apple, and Microsoft — more services are adding support all the time.

Prepare for account recovery — before you need it

The best time to set up account recovery is before something goes wrong. A few minutes now can save you from being permanently locked out later.

  • Recovery email — a second email address only you can access.
  • Recovery phone number — kept up to date, even if you change numbers.
  • Backup codes — download or print them, and store them somewhere safe (not in your email inbox).
  • Trusted devices — check which devices are currently trusted on your important accounts.
  • Emergency access — some services let a trusted contact help you regain access if you're ever locked out.
